ParkiCup is a gimbal-stabilised cup holder for people living with Parkinson's. Two pivoting rings decouple the drink from the tremor, a soft full-hand grip suits weaker fingers, and a foldable straw makes sipping possible without lifting at all.

The problem

A cup is designed for a hand that holds still.

Parkinson's brings tremor, muscle stiffness, slow movement and reduced grip strength. A standard cup answers none of that: the handle is small, the mass sits high, and every shake of the wrist travels straight into the liquid.

Spills follow. So does the quieter cost: people stop drinking in company, wait for help, or simply drink less. ParkiCup was designed so that the drink, not the person, does the adapting.

How it works

Five parts. One quiet idea.

1 2 3 4 5
01

Grip handle

A straight bar wrapped in soft black foam, held like a mug handle with the whole hand. No pinch grip, no small loop; the load sits close to the wrist.

02

Yoke

The D-shaped frame that plugs into the handle. Its two tips carry the pivot pins for the outer ring, so the whole gimbal hangs from the hand at one point.

03

Outer ring

Pinned to the yoke. It lets the cradle swing front to back while the hand tilts.

04

Inner ring

Pinned to the outer ring at 90°. Together the rings form a gimbal: two free axes, and gravity keeps the liquid level whatever the hand does.

05

Windowed cradle

A cone with a large window that takes an ordinary handleless cup. The window shows the cup and its base, keeps the weight down, and lets the cradle be lifted out for washing.

Design process

From sketch to a second prototype.

Every part was modelled, printed and assembled by the designer herself. The steps are shown with the current design; the last one is her own footage.

01CAD

CAD modelling

Handle, yoke, rings and cradle are drawn as separate bodies so each can be reprinted and tuned on its own.

02Render

Concept development

Renders check the balance point of the cradle and how far the rings can swing without touching the yoke.

03Slice

Print preparation

All five parts fit on one bed. Rings print flat for roundness; the handle prints upright for strength along the grip.

04Assemble

Prototype assembly

Rings snap onto their pins by hand. No screws, no glue, so a worn part can be swapped in seconds.

05Test

Assembly and test

Rings snap onto their pins, the yoke plugs into the grip. Held, tilted and shaken: the cradle finds level on its own.

Steady by design

What the prototype is, plainly.

Numbers below describe the first working prototype. They will be updated as testing with users continues.

Stabilisation
Two-axis passive gimbal, gravity-levelled cradle
Tremor addressed
Rest and action tremor in the 4–6 Hz band; wrist tilt front-back and side-side
Grip
Straight handle with soft foam sleeve, full-hand grip near the wrist
Drinking
Sip normally or through the foldable straw without lifting
Fits
Handleless cups and glasses; the cradle window shows the base
Parts
5 printed parts plus foam grip, tool-free assembly
Care
Rinse; the cradle lifts out of the rings for cleaning

About the designer

"How can design make everyday life easier for people living with Parkinson's?"

Selin is a student with a strong interest in health, design and innovation. ParkiCup began with that one question, and she carried it through alone: research, CAD, printing, assembly and testing.

The project rests on a simple belief: thoughtful design can improve small but meaningful moments, and health, engineering and empathy belong in the same room.
